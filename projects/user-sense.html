01

Design challenge

How might we design the sign-up and testing process for testers so it's efficient and easy to use, while giving them fast access to relevant tests and the information they need?
02

Context

User Sense is a Dutch UX research platform, founded in 2020, that helps companies improve their digital products through user testing. Its research runs on a self-built panel of more than 75,000 participants — people who sign up as "testers" to take part in studies.

My assignment, set as my graduation project, was to fix friction on the tester-facing side of that platform: signing up, finding and joining studies, and using the testing environment itself.

03

Research

I surveyed User Sense's own tester pool and reviewed support tickets to find out where the friction was actually concentrated, rather than guessing.

  • 46% of testers had hit a technical problem on the platform, most often around logging in.
  • Signing up for a specific test was the single most common complaint — ahead of participating in the test itself.
  • Baseline satisfaction was still a respectable 7.2/10, and most testers said they'd recommend User Sense to others. The platform wasn't broken — but the rough edges were costing it.

To see exactly where the experience dipped, I mapped it as a journey across five stages — sign-up, invitation, participation, evaluation, and repeat participation — scored on emotion at each step. The dip landed squarely on the invitation stage: unclear screener questions and testers not always making it through screening, echoing the survey finding that signing up was the biggest complaint.

04

Define

I turned the research into three personas, each representing a different way people use the platform:

  • A freelance marketer who gets stuck on unclear sign-up steps and never receives confirmation after registering.
  • A student who rarely sees tests that fit his profile, and doesn't understand why he's rejected when he does apply.
  • A working parent who avoids tests that require installing extra software, and wants clearer communication when she isn't selected.

Different life stages, same underlying problem: the platform wasn't telling people enough, early enough.

05

Design guidelines

I turned that into five principles before designing anything:

  • Clearer navigation and structure within the test environment.
  • Clear, visual instructions on where and how to complete a test.
  • Transparency around participation odds, through status updates instead of silence.
  • A stronger visual hierarchy for a more intuitive tester dashboard.
  • Onboarding that explains the process up front, to cut down on confusion later.
06

Concept exploration

Before committing to a direction, I designed and pitched three concepts to stakeholders: a redesigned study platform focused on fixing the existing sign-up flow, "Testify" — an onboarding flow that matches testers to studies through stated preferences, and a community-style app built around sharing insights.

The other two concepts had real strategic upside — deeper personalization, a fully integrated app experience — but both added technical complexity the team's capacity and User Sense's existing infrastructure couldn't realistically absorb yet. I recommended the first concept: it solved the two problems the research kept surfacing, transparency and ease of use, without requiring a technical rebuild, and stakeholders agreed it offered the fastest, lowest-risk path to real impact.

07

Final design

The flow starts before testers even open the app: an email notifies them the moment a matching study becomes available, with the key details — length, incentive, method — visible at a glance so they can decide whether to sign up without digging for information.

Passwordless sign-in replaced the password-based login entirely — testers get a one-time code by email instead, removing the single most common technical complaint.

The screener overview shows a profile-match percentage on every study, with clear "New," "Pending review," and "History" tabs — so testers always know where they stand instead of hearing nothing back.

The redesigned dashboard, shown at the top of this page, brings onboarding progress, available studies, and account balance together on one screen, instead of leaving testers to piece it together themselves. From there, testers can jump straight into a full studies overview — now available, scheduled, or history — and start or cancel a session in one click.

08

Outcome

The result was a redesigned tester environment — covering sign-up, the study dashboard, and the screener flow — validated through usability testing with real testers before handover to User Sense. Feedback on the new dashboard was clear: as one participant put it during testing, "Finally I have everything in one place — it makes the whole thing feel a lot calmer."

Testing also surfaced real, honest limitations — some labels weren't self-explanatory, and a few actions needed clearer confirmation feedback — the kind of refinement a next iteration would take on.

This case study reflects the design work from my graduation project; I don't have post-launch platform metrics to report here.
